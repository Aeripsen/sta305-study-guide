Part 0 · read once, everything later assumes itWhat are we actually doing, and what does every symbol mean

The one question this whole course asks

You have several groups. Their averages are different. But averages are always a bit different, just by luck. So the only real question is:

Is the gap between the group averages bigger than the random noise inside the groups?

That is it. Twelve units, one question. Everything else is bookkeeping about what counts as noise.

Why it is called Analysis of Variance when you are comparing means

You compare means by comparing two kinds of variance.
Take the spread inside each group: that must be pure noise, because everyone in a group got the identical treatment. Now take the spread between the group averages. If the treatment does nothing, that between-spread is also just noise, and the two numbers come out about the same. If the treatment does something, only the between-spread grows. So you divide one by the other and see if the ratio is near 1 or much bigger. That ratio is F.

Why not just do lots of t tests?

With a=5 groups there are 10 pairs. Ten tests at 5% each gives about a 40% chance of at least one false alarm.
That is the multiple comparisons problem, and it is why the course goes: one global F test first (is anything going on?), then, only if that rejects, a controlled follow-up (contrasts, Tukey, Bonferroni) to find out where. Units 3 and 4 are exactly these two halves.

The three sums of squares, in words

SST (Total) =\sum\sum(Y_{ij}-\bar Y_T)^2
All the variation there is. Take every single observation, measure how far it is from the overall average, square it, add up. Squaring is only so that pluses and minuses cannot cancel.
SSA (Treatment, "between") =\sum n_j(\bar Y_j-\bar Y_T)^2
The part the grouping explains. How far is each group's average from the overall average? The n_j is there because a whole group of n_j people sits at that average, so it counts n_j times.
SSE (Error, "within") =\sum(n_j-1)s_j^2
The part left over: the noise. How far is each observation from its own group's average. Since s_j^2 already measures that spread divided by n_j-1, multiplying back by n_j-1 recovers group j's raw sum of squares.
\[ SST=SSA+SSE \qquad\text{always. Total = explained + leftover.} \]

Why divide by df, and what df even is

MS=SS/df, and df = how many independent pieces of information went into that sum. Every mean you estimate from the data costs you one.
A sum of squares is bigger just because you added more terms, so you cannot compare SSA to SSE directly. Dividing by df turns each into an average, which makes them comparable. Treatment df is a-1 (a group means, minus 1 spent on the grand mean); error df is N-a (N observations, minus 1 per group for each group mean).
MSE is the single most important number on the page.
It is your estimate of \sigma^2, the background noise. It is the denominator of every F test in this entire course, in every design. When you learn a new design, the only thing that really changes is what got taken out of MSE first.

Blocking, Latin squares and covariates all do the same job: pull explainable variation out of the error term. Smaller MSE means a bigger F for the treatment, which means more power to detect a real effect. That is the entire motivation for Units 9 to 12, and it is worth being able to say in one line.

A school district is investigating the impact of device type (tablet, laptop) and study app (App A, App B, App C) on student test scores. Balanced design, 6 replicates per treatment. Participants randomly assigned. Scores recorded as a percentage.

(a) Identify the two factors. Are they crossed or nested? Be specific.
(b) List all the treatments.
(c) Identify the response and classify it as quantitative or categorical.
(d) Explain the importance of randomization in this study.

Solution A1

(a) Factor 1 is device type with 2 levels (tablet, laptop). Factor 2 is study app with 3 levels (A, B, C). They are crossed, because every level of device type appears with every level of study app, so all 2\times3 combinations exist.

Crossed vs nested
Crossed: every level of one factor occurs with every level of the other, so you can estimate an interaction. Nested: the levels of one factor are different within each level of the other (e.g. different teachers within each school), so no interaction is estimable. Ask: "is App A the same App A on both devices?" Yes, so crossed.

(b) The 2\times3=6 treatments are: tablet+App A, tablet+App B, tablet+App C, laptop+App A, laptop+App B, laptop+App C.

(c) The response is student test score as a percentage. It is quantitative (numeric and continuous).

(d) Randomly assigning participants means any uncontrolled nuisance variables, such as prior ability or motivation, are spread evenly across the six treatments rather than lining up with one of them. That removes systematic bias and is what allows a difference in scores to be attributed to the device and app rather than to a confounder.

Marking
"Be specific" means naming the actual levels from this problem, not saying "the two factors are crossed". Every part here wants the study's own words.

Solution A5
\[ F=\frac{MSA}{MSE}=\frac{SSA/(a-1)}{SSE/(N-a)} =\frac{\big(SSA/\sigma^2\big)/(a-1)}{\big(SSE/\sigma^2\big)/(N-a)} \]
This division by \sigma^2 is worth a mark on its own. His Test 1 key writes in the margin: "here the student must divide by \sigma^2. If not, minus 1 point." It changes nothing numerically but it converts each sum of squares into a chi-square.

B4 [4 marks] · the proof

One-way model y_{ij}=\mu_j+\varepsilon_{ij}, \varepsilon_{ij}\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2), equal sample sizes n. \bar Y_j=\frac1n\sum_i Y_{ij}, \bar Y=\frac{1}{na}\sum_j\sum_i Y_{ij}.

(a) Derive \mathrm{Cov}(\bar Y_j,\bar Y). (b) Derive \mathrm{Corr}(\bar Y_j,\bar Y). (c) Derive V(\bar Y_j-\bar Y).

Solution B4
The key realisation
\bar Y_j and \bar Y are not independent, because \bar Y contains every observation that \bar Y_j contains. Whenever one mean is nested inside another, you cannot just add variances. Write the big one as an average of the small ones and the covariance collapses.
(a) Write the grand mean as the average of the group means (valid because the design is balanced): \[ \bar Y=\frac1a\sum_{k=1}^{a}\bar Y_k \] \[ \mathrm{Cov}(\bar Y_j,\bar Y)=\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(\bar Y_j,\frac1a\sum_{k=1}^{a}\bar Y_k\right) =\frac1a\sum_{k=1}^{a}\mathrm{Cov}(\bar Y_j,\bar Y_k) \]
Covariance is linear in each argument; that is the bilinearity fact on the formula sheet.
By independence of different treatment groups, \mathrm{Cov}(\bar Y_j,\bar Y_k)=0 whenever k\neq j, so only the k=j term survives: \[ =\frac1a\,\mathrm{Cov}(\bar Y_j,\bar Y_j)=\frac1a V(\bar Y_j)=\frac1a\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{n} =\boxed{\frac{\sigma^2}{na}} \]
Different groups use disjoint sets of observations, hence independent, hence zero covariance. Say "by independence" here: that is where the mark is. And \mathrm{Cov}(X,X)=V(X).
(b) \[ \mathrm{Corr}(\bar Y_j,\bar Y) =\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(\bar Y_j,\bar Y)}{\sqrt{V(\bar Y_j)}\sqrt{V(\bar Y)}} =\frac{\sigma^2/(na)}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/n}\;\sqrt{\sigma^2/(na)}} \] \[ =\frac{\sigma^2/(na)}{\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\cdot\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{na}}} =\frac{\sigma^2/(na)}{\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\sqrt a}}=\frac{n\sqrt a}{na}=\boxed{\frac{1}{\sqrt a}} \]
Everything cancels except a. Sanity check: with a=1 the two means are the same thing and the correlation is 1. With many groups each one contributes less to the grand mean, so the correlation falls like 1/\sqrt a.
(c) Using V(X-Y)=V(X)+V(Y)-2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y): \[ V(\bar Y_j-\bar Y)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\frac{\sigma^2}{na}-2\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{na} =\frac{\sigma^2}{n}-\frac{\sigma^2}{na} \] \[ =\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\left(1-\frac1a\right)=\boxed{\frac{(a-1)\sigma^2}{na}} \]
The covariance term is what makes this smaller than the naive \sigma^2/n+\sigma^2/(na). Notice the covariance happened to equal V(\bar Y), which always happens when one mean sits inside the other.

\[ \mathrm{Corr}=\frac{\sum_j c_jk_j/n_j} {\sqrt{\sum_j c_j^2/n_j}\;\sqrt{\sum_j k_j^2/n_j}} \]
The \sigma^2 cancels. The numerator is exactly the orthogonality condition, so the correlation is zero precisely when the contrasts are orthogonal. That is what "orthogonal contrasts carry independent information" really means.

V1 Tukey HSD · on the sheet, never yet tested

Using the C1 data (means 62, 66, 52; n_j=5; MSE=28.3333; a=3, N-a=12), and given q_{0.05,3,12}=3.77:

(a) Compute the Tukey HSD critical difference. (b) Which pairs of treatments differ? (c) Give a 95% Tukey CI for \mu_1-\mu_3.

Solution V1
Tukey, once
Tukey controls the family-wise rate across all pairwise comparisons at once, using the studentized range distribution q rather than t. It is the most powerful option when you want every pair and the design is balanced. It does not handle general contrasts; for those you need Bonferroni or Scheffe.
\[ HSD=\frac{q_{\alpha,a,N-a}}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{MSE\left(\frac{1}{n_j}+\frac{1}{n_k}\right)} =\frac{3.77}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{28.3333\left(\tfrac15+\tfrac15\right)} \] \[ =2.6658\sqrt{28.3333(0.4)}=2.6658\sqrt{11.3333}=2.6658(3.3665)=\boxed{8.9744} \]
The q subscripts are \alpha, then a (number of groups, not a-1), then error df. Balanced here so every pair has the same HSD; unbalanced would need recomputing per pair.
(b) Compare each |\bar Y_j-\bar Y_k| to 8.9744: |62-66|=4 → not significant; |62-52|=10 → significant; |66-52|=14 → significant.
Treatment 3 differs from both 1 and 2; treatments 1 and 2 do not differ.
"If the observed difference exceeds the HSD value, the means differ." Report which pairs, in words.
(c) \[ (\bar Y_1-\bar Y_3)\pm HSD=10\pm8.9744=\boxed{(1.0256,\;18.9744)} \]
Zero is outside, consistent with (b). The Tukey CI is just the difference plus or minus the same HSD.
V2 Scheffe

Same data. After looking at the data you decide to test \psi_1=\mu_1+\mu_2-2\mu_3 (\hat\psi_1=24, s_{\hat\psi_1}=5.8310). Use Scheffe at 5%. Why is Scheffe the right choice here?

Solution V2
Scheffe, once
Scheffe controls the family-wise rate over every possible contrast simultaneously, including ones you invented after seeing the data. That is an infinite family, so it is the most conservative and least powerful. It is the only honest option for unplanned contrasts.
Scheffe critical value: \[ S=\sqrt{(a-1)F_{\alpha,\,a-1,\,N-a}}=\sqrt{2\times3.89}=\sqrt{7.78}=2.7893 \] Test statistic: \[ |t_{\text{obs}}|=\frac{|\hat\psi_1|}{s_{\hat\psi_1}}=\frac{24}{5.8310}=4.1160 \] Since 4.1160>2.7893, reject: the contrast is significant even by Scheffe.
Scheffe replaces the usual t_{\alpha/2,N-a} multiplier with \sqrt{(a-1)F}. Compare: the Bonferroni multiplier in C2 was 2.179 and Scheffe's is 2.7893, visibly harsher. That is the price of not planning ahead.

Why Scheffe: the contrast was chosen after seeing the data. Bonferroni and Sidak require the family to be fixed in advance, and Tukey only covers pairwise differences. Scheffe is the only procedure valid for a contrast you did not pre-specify.

V3 Helmert contrasts

For a=3 treatments, construct a set of Helmert contrasts and verify they are mutually orthogonal (balanced design).

Solution V3
Helmert construction, once
Compare group 1 to the average of all the rest. Then zero out group 1 and compare group 2 to the average of those after it. Continue. Formally: c_1=1 and c_j=-\frac{1}{a-1} for j>1; then for contrast j, set c_k=0 for k\le j-1, c_j=1, c_k=-\frac{1}{a-j} for k\ge j+1. It always produces a-1 mutually orthogonal contrasts.
\[ \psi_1:\ \mathbf c_1=\left(1,\ -\tfrac12,\ -\tfrac12\right) \qquad \psi_2:\ \mathbf c_2=(0,\ 1,\ -1) \] Both sum to zero, so both are contrasts. \[ \mathbf c_1\!\cdot\!\mathbf c_2=(1)(0)+\left(-\tfrac12\right)(1)+\left(-\tfrac12\right)(-1) =0-\tfrac12+\tfrac12=0 \] Orthogonal.
Only a-1=2 contrasts, which is the maximum size of a mutually orthogonal set. Their sums of squares add to SSA exactly.

V7 Proof variant · expected mean squares

(a) Show E[MSE]=\sigma^2. (b) State E[MSA] and explain in one sentence why this makes the F test work.

Solution V7
(a) Within group j, S_j^2 is unbiased for \sigma^2, so E\!\left[(n_j-1)S_j^2\right]=(n_j-1)\sigma^2. Summing over the groups: \[ E[SSE]=\sum_{j=1}^{a}(n_j-1)\sigma^2=(N-a)\sigma^2 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ E[MSE]=E\!\left[\frac{SSE}{N-a}\right]=\frac{(N-a)\sigma^2}{N-a}=\sigma^2 \]

(b) \[ E[MSA]=\sigma^2+\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{a}n_j\alpha_j^2}{a-1} \]
Under H_0 every \alpha_j=0, the second term vanishes, and both mean squares estimate \sigma^2, so F\approx1. V14 Two "why?" questions he writes on his own slides

(a) Why does every contrast have df=1?
(b) Why may a contrast be tested equivalently with a t test rather than an F test?
(c) Derive \alpha_{SB}=1-(1-\alpha_{FW})^{1/k}.

Solution V14

(a) His rule on the slide: df equals the number of equal signs in your system of tests. A contrast states one equation, \psi=0, so one equal sign, so df=1. Compare the global test \mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_a, which has a-1 equal signs and therefore a-1 df. This rule is worth remembering because it also explains why the interaction has (a-1)(b-1) df.

(b) Because t^2=F when the numerator has 1 df. Specifically t_{\text{obs}}=\hat\psi/s_{\hat\psi}\sim t_{N-a} and squaring it gives F_{1,N-a}. The two tests give identical decisions; the t version is more convenient if you also want a confidence interval or a one-sided test.

(c) If the k tests are independent and each uses level \alpha_{SB}, then each avoids a false positive with probability 1-\alpha_{SB}, so \[ P(\text{no false positive anywhere})=(1-\alpha_{SB})^k \] \[ \alpha_{FW}=1-(1-\alpha_{SB})^k \;\Longrightarrow\; (1-\alpha_{SB})^k=1-\alpha_{FW} \] \[ \Longrightarrow\; 1-\alpha_{SB}=(1-\alpha_{FW})^{1/k} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\alpha_{SB}=1-(1-\alpha_{FW})^{1/k}} \]

Two details he flags
n is the number in each group, written in capitals on all three of his slides, so multiply by k for the total. And unlike the Welch df (round down), a required sample size rounds up, because rounding down would leave you under the target power.

(c) Power depends on effect size, and a smaller effect is harder to detect. This is the practical reason small effects need very large studies, and it is the same point as "statistical significance is not practical significance" seen from the other side.

P8 [5 marks] · the proof

One-way model Y_{ij}=\mu_j+\varepsilon_{ij}, \varepsilon_{ij}\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2), i=1,\dots,n_j, j=1,\dots,a. Let \hat\psi=\sum_j c_j\bar Y_j with \sum_j c_j=0.

(a) Derive V(\bar Y_j). (b) Derive V(\hat\psi). (c) Hence state the distribution of \hat\psi and explain why the test statistic uses MSE rather than \sigma^2.

Show all steps and explicitly name the theorem or fact used at each step.

Solution P8
(a) \[ V(\bar Y_j)=V\!\left(\frac{1}{n_j}\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}Y_{ij}\right) \overset{\text{by independence}}{=}\frac{1}{n_j^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}V(Y_{ij}) =\frac{n_j\sigma^2}{n_j^{2}}=\boxed{\frac{\sigma^2}{n_j}} \]
The constant comes out squared. Independence is what lets the variance of a sum become the sum of variances, and saying so is worth a mark.
(b) \[ V(\hat\psi)=V\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{a}c_j\bar Y_j\right) \overset{\text{by independence}}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{a}c_j^2V(\bar Y_j) =\sum_{j=1}^{a}c_j^2\frac{\sigma^2}{n_j}=\boxed{\sigma^2\sum_{j=1}^{a}\frac{c_j^2}{n_j}} \]
Different treatment groups use disjoint observations, so they are independent and all covariance terms vanish. Each c_j comes out squared.
(c) Each \bar Y_j is normal, and a linear combination of independent normals is normal, so \[ \hat\psi\sim N\!\left(\sum_j c_j\mu_j,\;\sigma^2\sum_j\frac{c_j^2}{n_j}\right) \]
Name the fact. The mean follows from E[\bar Y_j]=\mu_j and linearity.
Because \sigma^2 is unknown, we replace it by its unbiased estimator MSE. Standardising with an estimated variance turns the normal into a t: \[ \frac{\hat\psi-\psi}{\sqrt{MSE\sum_j c_j^2/n_j}}\sim t_{N-a} \]
This is the same reason a one-sample z becomes a t. The df is N-a, the df of the MSE used in the denominator. Squaring gives F_{1,N-a}, which is why either test may be used.
